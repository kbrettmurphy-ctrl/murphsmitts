# MurphOS Photography Guidelines

**Version:** 1.1
**Status:** Draft
**Last Updated:** June 2026

---

# Why This Exists

Photography is one of the most important parts of the Murph's Mitts customer experience.

Photos show the work before the customer ever touches the glove.

They show texture.

They show patina.

They show care.

They show proof.

MurphOS uses photography to communicate craftsmanship, honesty, and trust without needing to over-explain.

A good photo should make the customer feel:

This glove was cared for.

This work is real.

This business pays attention.

---

# Photography Philosophy

Murph's Mitts photography should feel honest, warm, detailed, and intentional.

The goal is not to make every glove look new.

The goal is to show every glove at its best while respecting its character.

Used gloves should still look used.

Patina should remain visible.

Scars, creases, wear, and age are part of the story.

Photography should honor that story, not erase it.

---

# Core Principles

## Photos Are Proof

Photos are not decoration.

They prove the quality of the work.

They help customers understand what Murph's Mitts does.

They show the difference between neglected and cared for.

Every photo should have a reason to exist.

---

## The Glove Is the Subject

The glove should always be the focus.

Not the background.

Not the table.

Not tools.

Not filters.

Not dramatic editing.

The interface frames the glove.

The photo frames the glove.

Everything serves the glove.

---

## Honest Beauty

Murph's Mitts should make gloves look their best without making them look fake.

Do not over-edit.

Do not over-saturate.

Do not remove natural character.

Do not make old leather look artificially perfect.

The photo should feel polished, but believable.

---

## Consistency Builds Trust

A consistent visual style makes the brand feel organized and professional.

The site should not feel like a collection of unrelated photos taken in different places, lighting, and moods.

Different gloves may have different personalities.

The photography system should still feel unified.

---

# Visual Style

Murph's Mitts photography should feel:

* Warm.
* Clean.
* Textured.
* Calm.
* Honest.
* Detailed.
* Premium.
* Natural.
* Crafted.

It should not feel:

* Dark.
* Harsh.
* Cluttered.
* Over-edited.
* Artificial.
* Random.
* Cheap.
* Busy.
* Like a Facebook Marketplace listing with better intentions.

---

# Lighting

Lighting should be soft, natural, and even.

Good lighting makes leather texture visible without creating harsh glare or deep shadows.

## Preferred Lighting

Use:

* Soft natural window light.
* Indirect daylight.
* Diffused light.
* Soft side lighting.
* Consistent lighting across before and after photos.

## Avoid

Avoid:

* Harsh direct sunlight.
* Camera flash.
* Strong overhead garage lighting.
* Mixed warm and cool lighting.
* Deep shadows that hide detail.
* Bright glare on conditioned leather.
* Photos that look yellow, green, blue, or muddy.

Leather should look warm and natural, not radioactive.

Small ask, apparently.

---

# Backgrounds & Surfaces

Backgrounds should support the glove without competing with it.

The background should feel clean, simple, and intentional.

## Preferred Backgrounds

Use:

* Neutral warm surfaces.
* Clean workbench surfaces.
* Simple wood tones.
* Light cream or off-white backdrops.
* Dark navy or black surfaces when contrast is useful.
* Clean leather-working or glove-care context when intentional.

## Avoid Backgrounds

Avoid:

* Carpet.
* Grass.
* Truck seats.
* Car interiors.
* Random floors.
* Dirty work surfaces.
* Busy garages.
* Laundry rooms.
* Kitchen counters unless staged cleanly.
* Cluttered backgrounds.
* Anything that makes the photo feel accidental.

The customer should never wonder what else is happening in the room.

The answer should be nothing.

Because the glove is the point.

---

# Composition

Composition should make the glove easy to understand and satisfying to look at.

## Composition Rules

* Keep the glove as the clear subject.
* Leave breathing room around the glove.
* Avoid cutting off important parts unless it is an intentional detail shot.
* Use consistent angles for similar photo types.
* Show the glove's shape, web, pocket, and lace clearly.
* Use diagonal positioning when it helps the glove feel natural and dimensional.
* Avoid awkward crops that make the glove feel cramped.
* Keep the horizon and frame visually balanced.

## Preferred Angles

Use:

* Straight-on front view.
* Slight angled view.
* Pocket detail.
* Web detail.
* Heel and wrist detail.
* Lace detail.
* Palm and finger stalls when relevant.
* Side profile when structure matters.

## Avoid

Avoid:

* Random close-ups with no context.
* Photos where the glove shape is unclear.
* Extreme angles that distort the glove.
* Crops that cut off the web, heel, or fingers unintentionally.
* Too many photos that all show the same thing.

A photo should either show the whole glove or teach the customer something specific.

---

# Shot Types

Murph's Mitts should use a consistent set of photo types.

## Hero Shot

The hero shot is the strongest visual image.

Use it for:

* Home page.
* Service pages.
* Feature sections.
* Social posts.
* Gallery highlights.

Hero shots should have strong composition, clean lighting, and enough negative space for layout when needed.

The glove should feel important.

Not huge.

Important.

There is a difference, despite what every sports poster from 2007 tried to teach us.

---

## Full Glove Shot

A full glove shot shows the entire glove clearly.

Use it for:

* Gallery grids.
* Before and after sets.
* Order photos.
* Service examples.
* For-sale listings.

The customer should immediately understand the glove type, color, condition, and overall work.

---

## Detail Shot

A detail shot focuses on craftsmanship.

Use it for:

* New lace.
* Web work.
* Pocket condition.
* Leather texture.
* Knots.
* Heel lacing.
* Before/after leather condition.
* Patina.
* Problem areas.

Detail shots should show quality, not just closeness.

Close does not automatically mean useful.

The lens is not a microscope with a marketing degree.

---

## Process Shot

A process shot shows work in progress.

Use it for:

* About page.
* Process page.
* Social content.
* Behind-the-scenes storytelling.

Process shots may include tools, hands, lace, cleaner, conditioner, or workbench context.

The shot should still feel clean and intentional.

---

## Finished Glove Shot

A finished glove shot shows the completed work.

Use it for:

* Gallery.
* Customer updates.
* Social posts.
* Before and after sets.
* Service examples.

Finished glove photos should feel calm, clean, and proud without being overproduced.

---

# Before & After Photography

Before and after photos are powerful because they show transformation.

They must be honest.

## Before & After Rules

* Use the same angle when possible.
* Use similar lighting.
* Use similar distance.
* Use similar crop.
* Show the same side or detail.
* Do not make the before photo intentionally ugly.
* Do not make the after photo artificially perfect.
* Do not exaggerate the difference through editing.

Before and after photos should build trust.

They should not feel like a trick.

## Good Before & After

A good before and after shows:

* Cleaner leather.
* Better lace.
* Improved structure.
* Better shape.
* Healthier surface.
* Preserved character.

## Bad Before & After

A bad before and after relies on:

* Worse lighting before.
* Better lighting after.
* Different angles.
* Heavy saturation.
* Artificial contrast.
* Misleading edits.
* Crops that hide unresolved issues.

The work should sell itself.

If the photo has to lie, the photo failed.

---

# Editing Standards

Editing should make the photo look accurate, clean, and consistent.

Editing should not make the glove look fake.

## Allowed Editing

Use light adjustments for:

* Exposure.
* White balance.
* Contrast.
* Shadows.
* Highlights.
* Sharpness.
* Crop.
* Straightening.
* Minor background cleanup.

## Avoid Editing

Avoid:

* Heavy filters.
* Oversaturation.
* Artificial color shifts.
* Plastic-looking leather.
* Removing natural patina.
* Removing scars or wear that are part of the glove.
* Unrealistic sharpening.
* Vignette-heavy edits.
* Making every glove the same color tone.

The final image should look like the glove on its best day, not like it joined witness protection.

---

# Color Accuracy

Leather color should be represented honestly.

Conditioning can darken leather.

Cleaning can reveal original color.

Lighting can change everything.

Murph's Mitts photos should avoid misleading color changes.

## Color Rules

* Use consistent white balance.
* Avoid overly warm yellow casts.
* Avoid overly cool blue casts.
* Keep red, tan, brown, black, and navy lace accurate.
* Do not edit lace colors into something they are not.
* Do not make leather look richer than it actually is.

Customers should not be surprised when they see the glove in person.

---

# Lace Color Photography

Lace color photos are not gallery decoration.

They are customer decision support.

Customers use lace color photos to decide what they want on their glove, so these images must be especially clear, consistent, and color accurate.

A lace color photo should answer one question quickly:

What does this color actually look like?

## Lace Photo Rules

* Use the same lighting for every lace color.
* Use the same crop for every lace color.
* Use the same background for every lace color.
* Use the same angle and distance.
* Keep the lace color accurate.
* Avoid heavy shadows.
* Avoid glare.
* Avoid filters.
* Avoid dramatic edits.
* Avoid mixed photo styles in the same swatch grid.

Lace photos should feel like one unified set.

Not a hostage exchange between different cameras, different lighting, and different life choices.

---

## Lace Swatch Composition

Lace swatches should show enough leather to help the customer understand the color and texture.

The photo should prioritize:

* Color accuracy.
* Texture.
* Finish.
* Enough repeated lace surface to compare colors.
* Clean edges.
* Easy scanning.

Avoid lace photos where the color is hard to compare because the crop, lighting, or background changes too much from one color to another.

---

## Lace Color Labels

Lace color names should be visible and consistent.

Labels should not rely on the image alone.

If labels appear over photos, they must be readable and consistent across all swatches.

Preferred approach:

* Photo swatch.
* Color name below or beside the image.
* Availability status below the color name when needed.

Avoid inconsistent overlays where some colors have text on the image and others do not.

Avoid making customers guess what color they are looking at.

Guessing is friction wearing a tiny hat.

---

## Lace Availability Photography

Availability status is interface information, not photography.

Do not rely on the photo itself to communicate availability.

Use the interface to show:

* In stock.
* Low stock.
* Out of stock.
* Special order if applicable.

The photo should show the color.

The interface should show the status.

---

## Lace Photos on the Website

Lace colors should not feel buried.

They should appear wherever they help a customer make a decision.

Good locations include:

* Near relacing service information.
* Near relacing calls to action.
* Inside or near the service request form.
* In a dedicated lace color section.
* In any intake step where the customer chooses primary or secondary lace color.

The existing `/services/#lace-colors` anchor is useful, but an anchor alone is not the full solution.

Important choices should be surfaced where the customer is making the choice.

# Image Crops

MurphOS should use predictable image crops so the site feels organized.

## Gallery Grid

Use square crops.

```css
aspect-ratio: 1 / 1;
object-fit: cover;
```

The crop should keep the glove recognizable.

Avoid cutting off important glove details.

---

## Lace Color Grid

Use consistent square or near-square crops.

Recommended:

```css
aspect-ratio: 1 / 1;
object-fit: cover;
```

Each swatch should use the same crop logic so customers can compare colors quickly.

The goal is comparison, not drama.

---

## Editorial Images

Use wider crops for storytelling sections.

Recommended ratios:

```css
aspect-ratio: 4 / 3;
aspect-ratio: 16 / 10;
```

Editorial images should feel calm and spacious.

---

## Hero Images

Hero images may be wider and more cinematic.

Recommended ratios:

```css
aspect-ratio: 16 / 9;
aspect-ratio: 21 / 9;
```

Hero images should allow layout flexibility.

Leave negative space when text may appear over or near the image.

---

## Detail Images

Detail images may use tighter crops.

The crop should still make the detail easy to understand.

A customer should know what they are looking at without needing a treasure map and emotional support.

---

# Website Usage

Photography should be used intentionally across the public website.

## Home Page

Use strong, emotional, craftsmanship-focused images.

The home page should quickly communicate:

* Glove care.
* Quality work.
* Warmth.
* Trust.
* Attention to detail.

Avoid overwhelming the home page with too many competing images.

---

## Services Page

Use clear service-related images.

Show:

* Cleaning.
* Conditioning.
* Relacing.
* Finished glove examples.
* Lace details.
* Before and after examples when useful.

Service photos should help customers understand what they are paying for.

Lace color photography should appear close enough to relacing information that customers do not have to hunt for available options.

A dedicated lace color section may still exist, but it should not be the only place customers discover lace choices.

---

## Process Page

Use process and progress photos.

Show:

* Intake.
* Cleaning.
* Conditioning.
* Relacing.
* Final check.
* Return-ready glove.

The process page should make the customer feel reassured.

---

## Gallery Page

Use the best finished work.

Gallery photos should feel consistent, polished, and proof-driven.

The gallery should not include every photo ever taken.

Curate the work.

A gallery is not a junk drawer with pagination.

---

## About Page

Use photos that feel personal and trustworthy.

Good options include:

* Brett working on a glove.
* Workbench detail.
* Tools and lace.
* Gloves in progress.
* Finished gloves.
* Local, personal, or shop-context imagery if clean and intentional.

The About page can feel more human than the rest of the site.

Still clean.

Still careful.

Still not “random picture from Tuesday.”

---

# Customer-Submitted Photos

Customer-submitted photos are useful but should be treated differently than brand photography.

They may be messy, dark, or inconsistent.

That is acceptable for intake and admin use.

Do not let customer-submitted photos define the public visual standard unless they are strong enough.

## Use Customer Photos For

* Intake review.
* Order records.
* Before photos.
* Problem documentation.
* Customer communication.

## Public Use

Only use customer-submitted photos publicly if:

* Permission exists.
* Quality is acceptable.
* The glove is clear.
* The photo supports the brand standard.
* The photo does not include private or distracting background details.

---

# Admin Photography

Admin photos should prioritize usefulness.

They do not need to be perfect.

They need to be clear.

## Admin Photo Rules

* Show the issue clearly.
* Show the full glove when needed.
* Show detail areas when needed.
* Avoid blurry images.
* Avoid unnecessary duplicates.
* Keep photos tied to the correct order.

Admin photography is a work tool.

Customer-facing photography is a brand asset.

Different jobs.

Different standards.

---

# Social Media Photography

Social media can be slightly looser than the website, but it should still feel like Murph's Mitts.

Use social posts to show:

* Before and after.
* Process.
* Finished gloves.
* Lace color choices.
* Satisfying details.
* Local work.
* Behind-the-scenes moments.

Social photos may feel more immediate and personal.

They should not feel sloppy.

The brand does not get to take the day off because Instagram exists.

---

# Tools, Hands & Workbench

Tools and hands can add human character.

Use them intentionally.

## Good Uses

* Showing lace being pulled.
* Showing conditioner being applied.
* Showing knots or web work.
* Showing hand scale.
* Showing the craft in progress.

## Avoid

* Dirty tool clutter.
* Random mess.
* Distracting backgrounds.
* Tools becoming more important than the glove.
* Hands blocking the important detail.

The workbench should feel used, not chaotic.

There is a difference between craftsmanship and evidence of a minor explosion.

---

# For-Sale Glove Photography

Gloves listed for sale should be photographed clearly and consistently.

These photos need to help customers evaluate the glove.

## Required For-Sale Shots

Each for-sale glove should ideally include:

* Full front view.
* Full back view.
* Pocket view.
* Web detail.
* Palm detail.
* Heel/wrist view.
* Lace detail.
* Any notable wear or flaws.
* Strong primary listing photo.
* Optional hover photo.

## For-Sale Rules

* Show the glove honestly.
* Do not hide wear.
* Do not over-edit.
* Make the primary photo clean and clear.
* Use consistent crops for listing cards.
* Keep background simple.

For-sale photos should build confidence, not mystery.

Mystery is for crime documentaries, not used gloves.

---

# Alt Text

Photography should include useful alt text when published on the website.

Alt text should describe the image clearly.

## Good Alt Text

* Restored Rawlings baseball glove with tan lace.
* Close-up of new brown leather lace on a catcher's mitt.
* Before and after of a cleaned and relaced softball glove.
* Wilson first base mitt after cleaning and conditioning.
* Black leather lace swatch.
* Camel leather lace swatch.
* Royal blue leather lace swatch.

## Bad Alt Text

* Image.
* Glove.
* Nice glove.
* Photo 1.
* Before after wow.
* Lace.

Alt text should help users understand the photo if they cannot see it.

Accessibility is part of craftsmanship.

---

# File Quality & Performance

Images should look sharp but load quickly.

Large image files slow the site down.

Slow sites create friction.

Friction makes everyone worse, spiritually and technically.

## Image Rules

* Upload high-quality originals when possible.
* Compress images for the web.
* Avoid oversized files on customer-facing pages.
* Use responsive image sizes when possible.
* Keep crops consistent.
* Use descriptive filenames when practical.

Good photography should not make the site slow.

A beautiful image that takes forever to load is just a loading problem wearing makeup.

---

# Do / Don't Rules

## Do

* Use soft, natural light.
* Keep the glove as the subject.
* Show patina honestly.
* Use consistent crops.
* Curate the gallery.
* Show before and after honestly.
* Keep backgrounds simple.
* Let texture show.
* Use detail shots with purpose.
* Make photos feel intentional.
* Keep lace color photos consistent and accurate.
* Treat lace swatches as customer decision tools.

## Don't

* Over-edit leather.
* Hide natural character.
* Use cluttered backgrounds.
* Use harsh flash.
* Mix random photo styles everywhere.
* Make before photos intentionally worse.
* Use blurry customer photos publicly.
* Let tools overpower the glove.
* Fill the site with unnecessary images.
* Treat every photo as equally important.
* Bury important choice-support images where customers have to hunt for them.
* Mix inconsistent lace photo treatments in the same swatch grid.

---

# Photography Checklist

Before publishing a photo, ask:

1. Is the glove clearly the subject?
2. Is the photo sharp enough?
3. Is the lighting clean and natural?
4. Is the background simple?
5. Does the photo show useful detail?
6. Does the image feel honest?
7. Does it respect the glove's character?
8. Does it fit the Murph's Mitts visual style?
9. Is the crop appropriate for where it will appear?
10. Does this photo build trust?

For lace color photos, also ask:

1. Is the color accurate?
2. Does this swatch match the style of the other swatches?
3. Can the customer compare this color easily?
4. Is the color name clear?
5. Is availability shown by the interface, not hidden in the image?

If the answer to most of these is no, do not use the photo publicly.

# The Standard

Murph's Mitts photography should feel like the visual version of the work itself.

Careful.

Honest.

Detailed.

Warm.

Intentional.

A glove should never be photographed like a random object.

It should be photographed like something entrusted to Murph's Mitts for care.

The photo should show the glove's story, the quality of the work, and the respect behind the process.

Lace color photos should help customers make confident choices without searching, guessing, or second-guessing.

That is the MurphOS photography standard.
